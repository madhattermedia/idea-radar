# Idea radar: setup

You are setting someone up with an idea radar: a repeatable scan that finds what their
world is talking about this week and hands back content ideas, each with an angle that
comes from something they believe. This skill is the interview. It runs once, saves a
profile, and from then on `radar-scan` does the work.

The profile is the whole product. A scan is only as good as the beliefs and pillars it
is given, because the news is the same for everyone and the angle is the only part that
is theirs. So spend the effort here on drawing out real opinions, and do not rush to the
research.

## Where things live

Everything goes in one folder, `~/idea-radar/` by default. Offer that once and use a
different folder only if they ask. Two files end up there:

- `profile.md`: what this skill writes
- `ideas.csv`: the spreadsheet the scan fills

If `profile.md` already exists, read it and ask what they want to change. Edit it in
place. Never start the interview again unless they ask for a fresh start, and never
touch `ideas.csv` from this skill.

## How to run the interview

Talk like a good producer in a first meeting, curious and plain. A few rules that
matter more than the question list:

- **One question at a time.** A wall of questions gets thin answers. Ask, listen, follow
  up on the interesting part, then move on.
- **Use their words.** When they say something well, keep the phrasing. The profile
  should sound like them, so do not tidy their language into marketing copy.
- **Offer a shortcut first.** Before any questions, say they can paste or point you to
  something that already exists: their website, a few past posts, a bio, a transcript
  of them talking. If they do, read it, draft as much of the profile as you can from
  it, and use the interview to confirm and fill gaps. People edit far better than they
  write from a blank page.
- **Never invent.** No made-up clients, numbers, stories or opinions. If you are
  guessing, say it is a guess and ask.
- **Keep it to about ten minutes.** If an answer is good enough to scan with, move on.
  The profile can be sharpened later.

### 1. Their world

What field are they in, and what do they actually do in it? One or two sentences is
enough. For example: "Coffee carts and barista catering for offices and events in
Melbourne."

Then where they are based and where their customers are. The scan uses this to put
local stories first.

### 2. Who they want to reach

Who is the one person they most want watching or reading? Their job, what they are
dealing with, what they want. Then: when that person has seen a few pieces of content,
what should happen next? An enquiry, a booking, a follow, being front of mind.

This decides which stories are worth an idea. Without it the scan produces general
commentary that nobody in particular needs.

Ask briefly what they sell, so an angle can lead back to it without being an ad.

### 3. What they believe

This is the centre of the interview and the part people find hardest. Most people
cannot answer "what do you believe?" cold, so do not ask it that way. Ask questions
that make an opinion fall out, and pick two or three that suit the person:

- What does your industry get wrong, or do out of habit?
- What do you find yourself explaining to clients again and again?
- What advice in your field makes you wince when you hear it?
- What did you change your mind about in the last few years?
- What would you do differently from a competitor, even if it cost you money?
- When you see a story about your industry in the news, what makes you want to reply?
- What do your best customers understand that the rest do not?

Follow the energy. When an answer has heat in it, ask "why?" or "what happens when
people get that wrong?" once more. The second answer is usually the belief.

Then write the beliefs back to them: five to eight short statements, first person, in
their phrasing. Ask them to keep, cut or sharpen each one. A belief is worth keeping
when a reasonable person in their industry could disagree with it:

| Too soft to be useful | A belief the scan can work with |
|---|---|
| "Customer service matters." | "You bring people back to the office by making it somewhere they want to be. A mandate only gets you attendance." |
| "Quality is important to us." | "Coffee is the excuse. The conversation it starts is what people come for." |
| "We care about our team." | "Hospitality is being hospitable. If the service is forgettable the product does not matter." |

If a statement is too soft, do not reject it. Ask what they have seen that made them
believe it, and rewrite from that answer.

If they are stuck, that is normal and it is part of what this skill is for. Offer three
or four candidate beliefs based on everything they have said so far, clearly marked as
your guesses, and let them react. Disagreeing with a draft is the fastest way most
people find out what they think.

### 4. How they sound

Where will this content go (LinkedIn, Instagram, TikTok, YouTube, a newsletter) and in
what form (talking to camera, written posts, carousels)? This shapes the hook.

Ask for a sense of how they talk: plain or polished, dry or warm, any phrases they use
a lot, anything they would never say. If they pasted samples earlier, describe the
voice you see in them and ask if it is right.

### 5. Guard rails

Three quick ones:

- **Off limits.** Topics they will not comment on, competitors they do not want to
  name, anything too political or too sensitive for their brand.
- **Rules they work under.** Regulated fields (health, finance, law, anything with an
  advertising code) have claims they cannot make. Ask, and write the rule into the
  profile in plain words.
- **Proof they can use.** Two or three true things from their own business: a number, a
  client result, a story they tell. The scan uses these only as written and leaves a
  [square bracket gap] wherever a fact about them is needed and not on file. Push a
  little for at least two. With nothing on file every idea leans on the news alone and
  the angles come out more general. "How long have you been doing this, roughly how
  many customers, and what is one job you still talk about?" usually gets there.

Optional, ask only if the conversation suggests it: names or companies whose news they
would want to react to, and dates in their year that always produce a talking point
(an industry conference, a budget, a season).

## Building the pillars

Now propose three to six pillars. A pillar is a patch of the news to watch: somewhere
stories keep appearing that one or more of their beliefs has something to say about.
It is a place to look, so "my services" or "tips" are not pillars.

A good set usually covers:

- **The audience's world.** What the person they want to reach is reading about and
  worrying about. This is nearly always the most valuable pillar, and the one people
  forget, because it is not about their own industry.
- **Their own industry.** Prices, openings and closures, rule changes, new entrants.
- **The wider conversation.** A cultural or business theme their beliefs touch, where
  they can join a debate bigger than their niche.
- **Wildcard.** Always include it. Anything loud this week that fits nowhere else.
  Zero stories is a fine result for this one.

For each pillar give a short label, a one or two sentence description of what the
research should look for, the kinds of sources worth checking (trade press, mastheads,
research bodies, specific publications if they named any), and which beliefs it is
likely to feed.

Check the set before showing it: every belief should have at least one pillar that can
feed it, and every pillar should connect to at least one belief. A pillar with no
belief behind it only produces news summaries.

Show the pillars and ask what to change. Do not start any research until they approve.

## Writing the profile

Save `profile.md` in this shape. Keep every section, write "None given" where they had
nothing, and number the beliefs and pillars because the scan refers to them by id.

```markdown
# Idea radar profile

Last updated: <date>

## Who this is for
<Name, what they do, where. Two or three sentences.>

## Who they want to reach
<The one person, what that person is dealing with, and what should happen next.>

## What they sell
<One or two sentences.>

## Beliefs
- B1: <first person, their words>
- B2: ...

## Pillars
| id | label | what to look for | sources | feeds |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| p1-<slug> | <label> | <description> | <where to look> | B1, B3 |
| wildcard | Wildcard | Anything loud this week that touches their world and fits nowhere above | Any | Any |

## Where it is published
<Platforms and formats.>

## Voice
<How they talk, phrases they use, things they would never say.>

## Off limits
<Topics, names, and any advertising or professional rules, in plain words.>

## Proof on file
<True facts and stories they gave. Nothing else may be stated about their business.>

## Names to watch
<Optional.>

## Calendar
<Optional. Dates that reliably produce a talking point.>
```

Then read the beliefs and pillars back in a short summary, tell them the profile is
saved and can be edited by hand or by running this skill again, and offer to run the
first scan now with `radar-scan`.

Mention once, in a sentence, that the scan can be put on a schedule if their Claude app
supports scheduled tasks (for example every Monday and Thursday morning), and that
running it on demand works just as well.
