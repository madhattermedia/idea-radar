# Writing the hook

The hook is the first line the person says or writes. Its only job is to make the next
line necessary. These notes describe how a viewer's mind works. They are not a formula,
so do not turn them into one. If you can name the template you used, or the hook would
still work with the subject swapped out, start again from the idea.

## Write to one person

Picture the single person in "who they want to reach" and write as if this is being
sent to them directly. That removes most of what makes content sound like content: the
greeting, the warm-up, the explaining of things they already know.

Put the specifics in the thing being talked about, not in the audience. "If you are an
office manager" turns away more people than it hooks. "A $7 flat white" hooks anyone
who has bought one.

## Open a gap

Someone scrolling is weighing two things: is this worth it, and how long will it take.
The hook has to make the value feel large and the cost feel small.

Value comes from a gap between what they know and what the speaker knows. So the hook
leaves a question open that the viewer wants closed. It does not need to be clickbait
and it must not promise something the piece does not deliver. It needs to be a real
question, and it has to be in the viewer's head before any explaining starts.

## Lead with their take, not the news

The story is why this is timely. It is rarely the hook. "A new report came out this
week" is a journalist's opening. The hook comes from the belief: the claim, the
disagreement, or the thing the person has seen that the coverage missed. The story then
arrives in line two as the evidence.

A detail from the story can still sit inside a belief-led hook. "People are not upset
about a $5.17 coffee. They are upset about a $5.17 coffee from someone who did not look
up." The number is the story's. The claim is theirs.

## Be specific

Vague language is the most common failure and the cheapest to fix.

| Flat | Alive |
|---|---|
| "a while ago" | "45 days ago" |
| "prices have gone up a lot" | "a flat white just hit $7" |
| "many companies are doing this" | the named company and what it did |

In every hook, check whether a number, a name or a date could replace a general phrase.
Use only numbers and names that are in the story or in the profile's proof on file.

## Keep it simple enough to get in one pass

Read the hook as if a ten year old heard it with no context. If they would ask "what
does that mean?", rewrite the sentence, not the idea. Short sentences. A good hook
idea can be said in about ten words.

## Sound like them

Use the voice section of the profile. If they are dry, be dry. If they speak plainly,
do not hand them a polished line they would never say out loud. Read it aloud in your
head: if it sounds like an advert, it is wrong.

## Never

- Em dashes, en dashes, exclamation marks, emojis, hashtags.
- Stock openers and filler: "in today's fast-paced world", "game-changer", "here's the
  thing", "let that sink in", "unlock", "leverage", "harness", "delve", "paradigm
  shift", "the truth about", "nobody is talking about", "thoughts?", "agree?".
- A question the viewer can answer "no" to and keep scrolling.
- A claim about the person's own business that is not in the profile. Leave a
  [square bracket gap].
